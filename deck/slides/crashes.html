"Why does this matter? Because junk makes more junk. In 2009, a dead Russian satellite smashed into a working American one. That single crash created about two thousand two hundred new pieces of tracked junk. The International Space Station, with people on board, has had to move out of the way of junk forty-one times since 1999. And it's turned into a chain reaction: crashes now make new pieces faster than old ones fall back. The European Space Agency says the junk keeps growing even if we stopped launching tomorrow. What's at stake is everything satellites do for us: GPS maps, weather forecasts, TV and internet." Q&A backup: The chain reaction is called the Kessler syndrome (Kessler and Cour-Palais, 1978). A 2021 study (McKnight et al.) found that removing the fifty most dangerous derelicts, mostly old rocket stages, would cut their collision risk roughly in half.
