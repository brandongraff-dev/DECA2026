"Here's the good news: we don't have to remove all forty-seven thousand. More than half of them are small pieces from past crashes, far too many and too small to grab one by one. The real danger is about five hundred big dead rocket stages and satellites, mostly one to nine tonnes each. They're the bombs. When two of them collide, one crash can make thousands of new pieces, like the two thousand two hundred from 2009. Experts have ranked the fifty worst. Removing just those would cut the risk of junk-making crashes about in half. NASA's models say we need to remove about five big objects a year just to keep low orbit from getting worse. And how many big dead objects has any cleanup spacecraft removed so far? Zero. The first attempt to even grab one is planned for 2027." Q&A backup: One of our reusable trucks would do about 8 a year (our model), more than NASA's ~5 benchmark. "Zero so far" means no spacecraft has captured and removed a large derelict; ADRAS-J (2024) only inspected one, and ADRAS-J2 plans capture around 2027–28. Sources: ESA (more than half of tracked objects are fragments from 500+ breakups); McKnight, IAA memo (about 480 massive derelicts in LEO, about 810 tonnes); McKnight, Top 50 update presented at IAC 2025 (removing all 50 roughly halves debris-generating potential; the top 10 about 30%; reported by Ars Technica); Liou & Johnson, NASA (2009) and Liou (2011) (about 5 a year; NASA calls it a notional planning benchmark). About 8 a year = about 50 removals over about 6 years for one reused truck, our model. Also: the ISS has moved out of the way of junk 41 times since 1999 (NASA ODQN 29(3)). Honest limit: 34 of the 2025 top 50 are Russian/Soviet and 10 Chinese; about 30 are SL-16 and SL-8 stages. Our truck fits the 1.4-tonne SL-8s (a later route, with Russia's consent). The 9-tonne SL-16s need about 870 kg kits each, too heavy for a 10-stop truck, so they need a bigger tug or a partner.
