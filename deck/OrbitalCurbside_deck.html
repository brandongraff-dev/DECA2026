4

"Orbital Curbside is a space garbage truck. One electric spacecraft runs a route through a cluster of dead satellites or old rocket stages. At each stop it attaches a small rocket kit that drops the object into the atmosphere to burn up, then it drives on. Customers pay a fixed fee per object, when it's confirmed coming down. We serve space agencies, satellite operators and insurers. And the value is the price: our model says about twelve million dollars per removal, versus forty to ninety-three million in today's contracts." Q&A backup: Why "modelled"? Because no one has flown this yet. Every number comes from our open-source model, benchmarked against NASA JPL's competition-winning solution, with every assumption listed in the appendix.

Orbital Curbside

11

"Here's what makes us different: we don't chase the junk, we let Earth bring it to us. Earth bulges at the equator, and that bulge slowly swings every orbit around, at a speed that depends on height. So instead of burning fuel to turn toward the next target, our truck parks at a slightly different height and waits for the target's orbit to swing over to meet it." [Pause after the headline. Let it land.] Q&A backup: Is the drift trick new? No. It was used in GTOC9 (2017) and studied by Di Carlo, Vasile and others, and in a 2026 IAC paper. What's new here is pricing it as a customer-facing service on the live 2026 catalogue, with two products and a reusable truck.

V · Solution · How it works

Closing a 19° plane gap: burn now ≈ 1,094 kg of propellant; drift for a year ≈ 19 kg.

01

Park. Move to a slightly different height than the next target.

02

Drift. Earth’s bulge turns the two orbits at different speeds, so the gap closes for free.

03

Hop. A short electric burn to meet the target.

04

Tag. Attach a small deorbit kit that drops the object into the atmosphere. Drive on.

Orbital Curbside

13

"Four steps: park, drift, hop, tag. Park at a slightly different height than the next target. Drift while Earth's bulge closes the gap for free. Hop with a short, gentle electric burn. Tag the object with a small rocket kit that drops it into the atmosphere, then drive on. The chart shows why waiting matters. Closing a nineteen-degree gap right away takes about one thousand ninety-four kilograms of propellant, more than half the truck. Drifting for a year takes about nineteen." Q&A backup: The formula is the J2 nodal precession rate, dΩ/dt = −1.5·n·J2·(R/p)²·cos i. We correct it with second-order terms so it matches the SGP4 model used for real satellite tracking (slide 14).

"How do you know a high-schooler's math is right? I checked it against the best. In 2017, ESA ran a global competition to plan exactly this kind of route. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory won and published every leg. I rebuilt all one hundred thirteen. My total lands within two point two percent of theirs. Against SGP4, the model used to track real satellites, my orbit planes match within six hundredths of a degree. Q&A backup: Our model is about 2% optimistic, which is why the plan carries a 10% Δv margin. The four methods are beam search, simulated annealing, a genetic algorithm and ant colony optimisation.

16

"Now the money. With our truck removing just one object, our model says about ninety-six million dollars, right in line with today's contracts. That's our sanity check. Ten stops on one route: about twelve million per object. Across twenty thousand Monte Carlo runs, eighty percent land between eleven and fifteen million. Reuse the truck for ten routes: about four point four million per object. The biggest unknown is the truck's own cost, fifty to a hundred fifteen million, and that's what I'm working hardest to pin down." Q&A backup: Where does the money go on one route? Mostly truck hardware ($80M base), then kits ($15M for 10 at an assumed $1.5M each), then launch (~$15M). Propellant is under $0.2M. Electric vs chemical changes cost per removal by only ~1–3%; the levers are stops per route and reuse.

V · Solution · Risks and roadmap

What could stop us, and what we do about it.

Capture

Grabbing a tumbling stage hasn’t been done yet. We follow ADRAS-J2 and partner rather than invent the arm.

Cost

Truck and kit prices are estimates. We test them with experts and keep a 10% fuel margin.

Legal consent

Owners keep control of their objects. We start with customers’ own and partner-nation objects.

Re-entry safety

Heavy objects get a controlled re-entry over the ocean, not a slow fall.

"Our four biggest risks. Capture: nobody has grabbed a tumbling rocket stage yet, so we watch Japan's ADRAS-J2 and partner with whoever proves it. Cost: our prices are estimates, so I'm testing them with experts. Legal: owners keep control of their objects, so we start with customers' own and partner-nation objects. Safety: heavy objects get a controlled re-entry over the ocean. The roadmap: validate this year, sign a capture partner, fly a small pathfinder, then our first ten-stop truck from 2029." Our edge is the route and the pricing, not the arm. Dates after 2027 are targets, not commitments.
