6

"Here's the good news: we don't have to remove all forty-seven thousand. More than half of them are small pieces from past crashes, far too many and too small to grab one by one. The real danger is about five hundred big dead rocket stages and satellites, mostly one to nine tonnes each. They're the bombs. When two of them collide, one crash can make thousands of new pieces, like the two thousand two hundred from 2009. Experts have ranked the fifty worst. Removing just those would cut the risk of junk-making crashes about in half. NASA's models say we need to remove about five big objects a year just to keep low orbit from getting worse. And how many big dead objects has any cleanup spacecraft removed so far? Zero. The first attempt to even grab one is planned for 2027." Q&A backup: One of our reusable trucks would do about 8 a year (our model), more than NASA's ~5 benchmark. "Zero so far" means no spacecraft has captured and removed a large derelict; ADRAS-J (2024) only inspected one, and ADRAS-J2 plans capture around 2027–28. Sources: ESA (more than half of tracked objects are fragments from 500+ breakups); McKnight, IAA memo (about 480 massive derelicts in LEO, about 810 tonnes); McKnight, Top 50 update presented at IAC 2025 (removing all 50 roughly halves debris-generating potential; the top 10 about 30%; reported by Ars Technica); Liou & Johnson, NASA (2009) and Liou (2011) (about 5 a year; NASA calls it a notional planning benchmark). About 8 a year = about 50 removals over about 6 years for one reused truck, our model. Also: the ISS has moved out of the way of junk 41 times since 1999 (NASA ODQN 29(3)). Honest limit: 34 of the 2025 top 50 are Russian/Soviet and 10 Chinese; about 30 are SL-16 and SL-8 stages. Our truck fits the 1.4-tonne SL-8s (a later route, with Russia's consent). The 9-tonne SL-16s need about 870 kg kits each, too heavy for a 10-stop truck, so they need a bigger tug or a partner.

"So where do we start? With ten old rocket stages from the US, India and France. We picked them for two reasons. First, they sit in the orbit band where cheap, shared SpaceX launches already go, so our truck makes its first pickup just eighty days after launch. Second, no permission problem. Under space law, every object still belongs to the country that launched it. These belong to countries whose space agencies already pay for cleanup. The bigger prize comes later: a hundred forty-two Soviet-era rocket stages, the same type that shows up on experts' list of the fifty most dangerous objects. But Russia must agree, and getting there takes a year of thrusting, so those cost about thirteen million each." Q&A backup: The stages: 4 Indian PSLV, 4 French Ariane and 2 US (Agena, Delta) upper stages, 600–1,900 kg each, in the sun-synchronous band at 650–850 km. Legal basis: Outer Space Treaty, Article VIII (state of registry keeps jurisdiction and control). Are these the most dangerous objects? No, and say so: the first route is chosen for consent and cheap access, to prove the truck works. The most-dangerous list is mostly Russian/Soviet (34 of 50) and Chinese (10); 11 SL-8 stages were on the 2021 list (McKnight et al. 2021).

We don't chase the junk. We let Earth bring it to us.

Propellant to close a 19° gap between two orbits

1,094 kg

Wait about a year

19 kg

57× less. Our planner trades waiting time against fuel to find the cheapest route.

Our model · J2 nodal precession (Vallado, 2013)

11

"So how can one truck reach ten objects cheaply? Here's the secret: we don't chase the junk. We let Earth bring it to us. Every orbit slowly swings around Earth, like the hand of a clock. And lower orbits swing faster, because Earth isn't a perfect ball; it bulges at the equator. So our truck parks a little higher or lower than its next target, and simply waits until the two line up, like the hands of a clock meeting. The difference is huge. Turning with fuel to close one of these gaps would take about a thousand kilograms of propellant. Waiting a year takes about nineteen." Q&A backup: The swing is called nodal precession, caused by Earth's oblateness (the J2 term): dΩ/dt = −1.5·n·J2·(R/p)²·cos i. Our planner prices waiting time against fuel, so routes aren't just fuel-optimal; they're cost-optimal.

"Here's one trip, in four steps. One: the truck rides a shared SpaceX rocket, the cheapest seat to space. Two: it climbs to the junk's height and drifts, letting Earth line up the next target. Three: a robot arm holds the old rocket, and the truck latches on a small rocket kit. Four: the kit fires, the old rocket falls into the atmosphere and burns up, and the truck drives on to the next stop. Ten times per trip." Q&A backup: The kit lowers the stage's lowest point to 250 km so it re-enters within months; heavy objects get a controlled re-entry over the ocean. Kits weigh about 85 kg each on our first route. The arm and capture are the least-proven part; Japan's ADRAS-J2 plans to demonstrate capture around 2027–28.

"How do you know a high-schooler's math is right? I checked it against the best. In 2017, the European Space Agency held a contest to plan exactly this kind of route. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory won, and published every move. I rebuilt all one hundred thirteen. My fuel totals land within two point two percent of theirs. "Now the money. Every number here includes the launch. Today's missions cost forty to ninety-three million per piece.
