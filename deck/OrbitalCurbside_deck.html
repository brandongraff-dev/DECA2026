"Why does this matter? Because junk makes more junk. In 2009, a dead Russian satellite smashed into a working American one. That single crash created about two thousand two hundred new pieces of tracked junk. The International Space Station, with people on board, has had to move out of the way of junk forty-one times since 1999. "Who pays to take out the trash? Three customers, in the order they'll pay. First, space agencies: Europe, Japan, the UK and the US. Their old rockets are up there, and they're already signing cleanup contracts. Second, satellite companies like Iridium and OneWeb. They must bring dead satellites down within five years, and some will fail. But listen to Iridium's CEO: at millions per satellite, he said, it doesn't make sense. They have budgets now. Companies own their satellites, which makes them legally simple, but they're price-sensitive, so they're early design partners. 24 dead first-generation Iridium satellites are still in orbit (Oct 2026 data); a 10-stop route through them models ≈$12.5M each. Rocket Lab agreed to buy Iridium in June 2026 (≈$8B).

"Here's what makes us different. Today, every piece of junk gets its own custom mission, at forty to ninety-three million dollars each. We run a route instead. One truck, one launch, ten stops. Because the expensive parts, the spacecraft and the launch, are shared by ten pickups, our model comes out at about twelve million dollars per piece. We know of no company selling a priced, multi-stop route on today's catalogue; we'd welcome being corrected.

IV · Unique Value Proposition · The secret

We don't chase the junk.
We let Earth bring it to us.

Every orbit slowly swings around Earth, like the hand of a clock. Lower orbits swing faster, because Earth bulges at the equator.

So the truck parks a little higher or lower than its next target and waits until the two line up, like clock hands meeting.

Turn with fuel

1,094 kg

of propellant

Wait a year

19 kg

of propellant

Fuel to close a 19° gap between two orbits, our model · J2 nodal precession (Vallado, 2013)

11

"So how can one truck reach ten objects cheaply? Here's the secret: we don't chase the junk. We let Earth bring it to us. Every orbit slowly swings around Earth, like the hand of a clock. And lower orbits swing faster, because Earth isn't a perfect ball; it bulges at the equator. So our truck parks a little higher or lower than its next target, and simply waits until the two line up, like the hands of a clock meeting. The difference is huge. Turning with fuel to close one of these gaps would take about a thousand kilograms of propellant. Waiting a year takes about nineteen." Q&A backup: The swing is called nodal precession, caused by Earth's oblateness (the J2 term): dΩ/dt = −1.5·n·J2·(R/p)²·cos i. Our planner prices waiting time against fuel, so routes aren't just fuel-optimal; they're cost-optimal.

V · Solution · How it works

Four steps, ten times per trip.

01

Ride along

The truck rides a shared SpaceX rocket, the cheapest seat to space.

02

Drift into line

It climbs to the junk's height and waits for Earth to line up the next target.

03

Grab and tag

A robot arm holds the old rocket. The truck latches on a small rocket kit.

04

Send it down

The kit fires. The old rocket falls and burns up. The truck drives to the next stop.

Renders from our 3D concept model, sized by our math. Not a flight design.

12

"Here's one trip, in four steps. One: the truck rides a shared SpaceX rocket, the cheapest seat to space. Two: it climbs to the junk's height and drifts, letting Earth line up the next target. Three: a robot arm holds the old rocket, and the truck latches on a small rocket kit. Four: the kit fires, the old rocket falls into the atmosphere and burns up, and the truck drives on to the next stop. Ten times per trip." Q&A backup: The kit lowers the stage's lowest point to 250 km so it re-enters within months; heavy objects get a controlled re-entry over the ocean. Kits weigh about 85 kg each on our first route. The arm and capture are the least-proven part; Japan's ADRAS-J2 plans to demonstrate capture around 2027–28.

"This is the truck, from our 3D model. At launch it weighs about one thousand six hundred fifty kilograms, about as heavy as a car. It has electric engines that run on xenon gas and are about five times more fuel-efficient than chemical rockets. A robot arm to hold each old rocket steady. Ten rocket kits, one for each pickup. Q&A backup: Mass budget (first route): kits 852 kg, dry truck 600 kg, xenon 198 kg. Two Hall thrusters, 0.202 N each class, Isp 1,710 s. "5× more efficient" compares specific impulse (≈1,710 s vs ≈300 s for a chemical engine). An electric truck can run about five routes before its thrusters wear out.

14

"How do you know a high-schooler's math is right? I checked it against the best. In 2017, the European Space Agency held a contest to plan exactly this kind of route. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory won, and published every move. I rebuilt all one hundred thirteen. My fuel totals land within two point two percent of theirs. Each dot here is one move; on the line is a perfect match. I also checked my orbit predictions against SGP4, the model used to track real satellites. They match within six hundredths of a degree." Q&A backup: 0.06° median on the SL-8 route; 0.30° on our first route. Our model is about 2% optimistic, so plans carry a 10% fuel margin. Four search methods (beam search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithm, ant colony) compete to pick the best of 2.4×10²¹ possible routes.

"Now the money. Every number here includes the launch. Today's missions cost forty to ninety-three million per piece. As a sanity check, if our truck picked up just one piece, our model says ninety-seven million: right in line with today. But with ten pickups on one trip, it drops to about twelve million per piece. And if we keep the truck and just send up new kits, about five million, for around fifty pickups. Out of twenty thousand simulated cases, eighty percent land between eleven and fifteen million. The biggest unknown is what the truck itself costs, and that's what I'm working hardest to pin down." Q&A backup: Where the money goes on one route: truck $80M, kits $15M (10 × assumed $1.5M), launch ≈$12.5M ($7,500/kg × 1,650 kg), operations ≈$7M, insurance ≈$7M. Price at a 20% margin: ≈$15.3M. A dedicated rocket instead of a rideshare adds $4–7M per piece.

"What could stop us? Four things, and here's what we do about each. Grabbing it: nobody has grabbed a tumbling old rocket yet.
